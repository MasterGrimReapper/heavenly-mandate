The immortals

Fifteen heroes of Chinese myth and history, in six roles. Each one fights with four abilities and a passive, and summons a troop of their own. Their full legends unlock in the game’s Codex as you master them.

悟

Sun Wukong

孙悟空Sūn Wùkōng

The Monkey King

武Fighter
猴Troop: Monkey Warriors

Born from stone, he stormed Heaven itself with a staff that grows to any size.

Abilities and legend
  • 变Seventy-Two TransformationsPassiveEvery fourth basic attack is a guaranteed critical strike.
  • 击Ruyi StrikeDamageThrust the Ruyi Jingu Bang forward, extending it to strike every foe in a line.
  • 旋Staff WhirlwindControlSpin the staff around you, battering and slowing every enemy nearby by 35%.
  • 云Cloud SomersaultMobilityLeap onto a cloud toward the cursor and land in Golden Body, gaining a shield.
  • 柱Pillar of HeavenUltimateThe staff grows to the size of a sky pillar and slams down, knocking up everything beneath.
  • 猴Monkey WarriorsTroopPluck a tuft of hair and blow it into five monkey warriors armed with little staffs.
“Heaven itself cannot hold me!”

Born of stone

On the Mountain of Flowers and Fruit a stone egg, warmed by heaven and earth since the world began, split open in the wind. A monkey stepped out and bowed to the four directions, and light shot from his eyes to the Jade Emperor's palace. He leapt through the Water Curtain waterfall first of all the monkeys and became their king.

Sources: Journey to the West (Xiyou ji), attributed to Wu Cheng'en, 1592: chapters 1–2 (birth and training), 3 (the staff), 4–7 (Havoc in Heaven and the Buddha's palm), 14 (freed by Tang Sanzang), 100 (made a Buddha). The Monkey King may draw on the white ape of older tales and on Hanuman of the Ramayana; scholars still argue which.

The third eye

Erlang Shen, the Second Son, is a nephew of the Jade Emperor who chose to live apart from Heaven at Guankou. In the middle of his forehead is a third, vertical eye that sees through every disguise and every illusion.

Sources: Journey to the West, chapter 6: the battle with the Monkey King. Investiture of the Gods: Yang Jian and the hound.

The Peach Garden

In a peach garden in Zhuo, under blossoming trees, three men swore to be brothers: Liu Bei, a sandal-maker of the imperial line; Zhang Fei, a butcher; and Guan Yu, a fugitive with a long beard and a face red as a ripe date. They asked to die on the same day, though they were born on different ones.

Sources: Records of the Three Kingdoms (Sanguozhi), Chen Shou, 3rd century: the historical Guan Yu. Romance of the Three Kingdoms (Sanguo yanyi), attributed to Luo Guanzhong, 14th century: chapter 1 (the Peach Garden oath), 5 (the warm wine), 25–27 (the five passes), 77 (his death).

“Is it dinner time yet?”

Marshal of the Heavenly Canopy

He was once Marshal Tianpeng, commander of eighty thousand sailors on the River of Heaven. At the Peach Banquet he drank too much and flirted with Chang'e, and the Jade Emperor had him beaten and thrown down to earth.

Sources: Journey to the West, chapters 8, 18–19 (Gao Village) and 100. Marshal Tianpeng was an older Taoist war god before the novel made him a pig.

The Crystal Palace

Four Dragon Kings rule the four seas, and the eldest, Ao Guang, rules the Eastern Sea from a palace of crystal on the sea floor. Shrimp soldiers and crab generals guard its gates, and its treasury holds weapons forged for the gods.

Sources: Journey to the West, chapter 3: the Monkey King takes the staff. Investiture of the Gods, chapters 12–14: Nezha and Ao Bing.

Seven sworn brothers

When Sun Wukong was a young king on the Mountain of Flowers and Fruit he swore brotherhood with six demon kings, and the Bull Demon King was the eldest. While Wukong called himself Great Sage Equal to Heaven, the Bull named himself Great Sage Who Pacifies Heaven.

Sources: Journey to the West, chapter 3 (the seven brothers), chapters 40–42 (Red Boy) and chapters 59–61 (the palm-leaf fan and the battle).

Three years in the womb

Lady Yin carried her third child for three years and six months, and gave birth to a ball of flesh. Her husband, the general Li Jing, split it with his sword, and out jumped a boy wearing a golden bracelet, the Universe Ring, and a red silk sash, the Armillary Sash. The immortal Taiyi Zhenren took him as a disciple.

Sources: Investiture of the Gods (Fengshen yanyi), attributed to Xu Zhonglin, 16th century: chapters 12–14. Journey to the West, chapter 4: Nezha fights the Monkey King with three heads and six arms.

White Tiger Ridge

On a steep ridge on the road west lived a demon who was nothing but a heap of old bones that had gathered spirit. She had heard what every demon had heard: whoever eats the flesh of the monk Tang Sanzang will live forever.

Sources: Journey to the West, chapter 27, “The corpse demon three times tricks Tang Sanzang; the holy monk in anger dismisses the Handsome Monkey King”, and chapters 28–31. The Shaoxing opera film Monkey King Thrice Defeats the White Bone Demon (1960) made her one of the best-known villains of the novel.

The elixir

When the archer Hou Yi shot down nine of the ten suns, the Queen Mother of the West gave him an elixir of immortality. There was enough for two to live long, or for one to rise to Heaven. His wife Chang'e drank all of it.

Sources: Huainanzi, “Peering into the Obscure” (Lanming), 2nd century BCE: the elixir and the flight. Miscellaneous Morsels from Youyang (Youyang zazu), Duan Chengshi, 9th century: Wu Gang and the osmanthus.

The Sleeping Dragon

Zhuge Liang, courtesy name Kongming, farmed and studied in a thatched cottage at Longzhong and compared himself to the great ministers of the past. People called him the Sleeping Dragon. Liu Bei visited three times before he was received; then Zhuge laid out the plan that would divide the empire in three.

Sources: Records of the Three Kingdoms: the historical chancellor of Shu Han (181–234). Romance of the Three Kingdoms: chapters 37–38 (the three visits), 46 (borrowing arrows), 49 (the east wind), 95 (the empty fort, a story the novel invented).

A thousand years of practice

A white snake spirit spent a thousand years in cultivation on Mount Emei until she could take a woman's form. She called herself Bai Suzhen, and with her companion, the green snake Xiaoqing, she went down to the West Lake at Hangzhou in spring.

Sources: “Madam White Is Kept Forever Under Leifeng Pagoda”, in Stories to Caution the World (Jingshi tongyan), Feng Menglong, 1624. Later operas and novels made her a heroine rather than a demon; the Legend of the White Snake is one of China's Four Great Folktales.

Ten suns

Di Jun, the god of the eastern sky, had ten sons, and each was a sun. They took turns crossing the sky, one a day, in the branches of the Fusang tree. Then one day all ten rose together. The crops withered, the rivers boiled, and the people cried out.

Sources: Huainanzi, “The Basic Warp” (Benjing), 2nd century BCE: the ten suns and the monsters. Questions of Heaven (Tianwen) in the Songs of Chu, 4th–3rd century BCE.

The weaver at the door

Click, click, the ballad begins: Mulan weaves at the door. Then the loom falls silent and she sighs. Last night she saw the draft notice; the Khan calls up his army, and her father's name is on every scroll. He has no grown son, and she has no elder brother.

Sources: The Ballad of Mulan (Mulan ci), probably from the Northern Wei (5th–6th century), preserved in the Music Bureau Collection (Yuefu shiji) of Guo Maoqian, 12th century. The surname Hua comes later, from Xu Wei's Ming play The Female Mulan.

The silent girl

Lin Moniang was born in 960 on Meizhou Island, off the coast of Fujian, the daughter of a fisherman. She did not cry once in her first month, so she was called Moniang, “silent girl”. She learned to swim, to read the weather and to heal.

Sources: Song dynasty records of Meizhou Island and the first imperial titles (12th century). The Qing court named her Tianhou in 1684; Mazu belief and customs were inscribed by UNESCO in 2009.

Forty years on Kunlun

Jiang Shang studied the Way on Mount Kunlun under the Primeval Lord of Heaven for forty years, and never quite became an immortal. At seventy-two his master sent him down the mountain: his fate was to help a new dynasty replace the cruel king of Shang, and to name the gods.

Sources: Records of the Grand Historian (Shiji), Sima Qian, c. 94 BCE, chapter 32, the House of Taigong of Qi. Investiture of the Gods (Fengshen yanyi), 16th century: chapters 15, 23–24 (the straight hook and King Wen) and 99 (the investiture).
